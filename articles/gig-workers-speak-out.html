Gig Workers Speak Out: The Hidden Injuries Behind Canada's Delivery Economy

Twelve delivery drivers share their experiences with injuries, wage theft, and the fight for basic workplace protections.

MC
By Maria Chen
March 21, 2026 · 10 min read · Labour
⚠ Content Advisory

This article contains descriptions of workplace injuries, including car accidents and physical assaults. It also discusses financial hardship and mental health impacts. If you are a gig worker experiencing unsafe conditions, the Workers' Action Centre can be reached at 416-531-0778.

Priya Kapoor was three hours into a Friday night shift for RapidDrop, one of Canada's fastest-growing food delivery platforms, when a driver ran a red light at the intersection of Dundas and Ossington in Toronto and hit her bicycle at roughly 50 kilometres an hour. She was thrown six metres. Her left femur fractured in two places. Her helmet cracked. The bag of pad thai she was delivering landed in the gutter, untouched.

From the ambulance, unable to move her leg, Kapoor, 27, did something that would have seemed absurd in any other job: she opened the RapidDrop app and marked the order as undeliverable. If she didn't, the algorithm would penalize her reliability score, pushing her further down the queue for future orders. The customer's $4.50 delivery fee would be refunded. The tip — $2.00 — would disappear. Kapoor would receive nothing for the order, and nothing for the three hours she had already worked.

"I'm lying in the ambulance and I'm thinking about my rating," Kapoor said, three months later, from the living room of the apartment she shares with two roommates in Scarborough. Her leg was still in a brace. She had not worked since the accident. She had no workplace insurance, no disability benefits, and no sick pay. RapidDrop's response to her support ticket about the accident, which she showed us, read: "We're sorry to hear about your experience. As an independent contractor, you are responsible for your own safety and insurance. We recommend consulting with your personal insurance provider."

"My personal insurance provider," she repeated, with a laugh that wasn't really a laugh. "I'm a delivery driver making $14 an hour before gas and maintenance. I don't have insurance."

214,000
Canadians work primarily as gig-based delivery or ride-hail drivers, according to Statistics Canada's 2025 Labour Force Survey. Fewer than 3 percent have access to workplace injury compensation through their platforms.

Twelve Drivers, Twelve Stories

Over the past three months, Impact Narrative Media spoke with 12 delivery drivers working across four platforms in Toronto, Vancouver, Calgary, and Montreal. Their experiences, while individual, share a pattern: physical danger without protection, financial precarity masked as "flexibility," and an algorithmic management system that treats human beings as interchangeable units of delivery capacity.

Ahmed Hassan, 34, Calgary. A former IT technician in Khartoum, Sudan, Hassan has been driving for FoodFleet since arriving in Canada in 2022, while he waits for his professional credentials to be recognized. Last November, he was assaulted by a customer who accused him of taking too long. The man punched Hassan in the face, breaking his nose. Hassan called police, who took a report. He called FoodFleet, which deactivated the customer's account. He asked if the platform would cover his medical expenses — an X-ray, pain medication, and a follow-up visit totalling $340. "They said no. They said I'm not an employee. They said they'd give me a $25 credit for my next order."

Jasmine Tran, 23, Vancouver. Tran rides an e-bike for three different delivery apps simultaneously, toggling between them to maximize her earning potential. She typically works 10-hour shifts, six days a week. Over the past year, she has developed chronic pain in her right wrist and shoulder from repetitive motions — carrying bags, gripping handlebars, reaching into warming bags. "I went to a walk-in clinic and the doctor asked what kind of work I do. When I said gig delivery, he said, 'Well, have you considered doing less?' That was his medical advice. Do less." Tran cannot do less. Her rent is $1,850 a month. Her average net earnings, after app fees, equipment costs, and phone charges, work out to $15.20 an hour.

Daniel Osei, 41, Toronto. Osei drives a car for two delivery platforms and a ride-hail service. In January 2026, he was rear-ended while stopped at a traffic light with a food order in the car. His car sustained $4,200 in damage. His personal auto insurance denied the claim because he was using the vehicle for commercial purposes without a commercial policy — a policy that would cost him an additional $3,800 per year, roughly a quarter of his net delivery earnings. The platform's insurance, which it advertises as a benefit, covered only third-party liability, not damage to Osei's vehicle. He borrowed money from his brother to repair the car and was back on the road within a week. "I couldn't afford not to be."

"The app doesn't know I'm a person. It knows I'm a dot on a map that moves food from one location to another. When the dot stops moving, it finds another dot."

The Algorithm as Boss

Every driver we spoke with described the same phenomenon: management by algorithm. There is no human supervisor, no office to visit, no number to call that connects to a decision-maker. The app assigns orders, sets pay rates, monitors performance, and disciplines workers — all automatically, all opaquely.

"The app doesn't know I'm a person," said Marcus Webb, 29, a Toronto bicycle courier who has worked for three platforms over four years. "It knows I'm a dot on a map that moves food from one location to another. When the dot stops moving, it finds another dot."

Several drivers described what they call "tip-baiting" — customers offering a large tip to attract a driver to a distant or difficult delivery, then reducing or eliminating the tip after delivery. On most platforms, drivers can see the promised tip before accepting an order. Tip-baiting wastes their time and fuel on a delivery they would not have accepted at the actual pay rate. Two platforms we contacted for comment said they have policies against tip-baiting but declined to share enforcement data.

More pervasive is what labour researchers call "algorithmic wage theft." Dr. Valerio De Stefano, a labour law professor at York University, has studied how delivery platforms structure pay. "The base pay per delivery has dropped by approximately 30 percent across major platforms since 2021," De Stefano told Impact Narrative Media. "But the way it's presented in the app — bundled with tips, surge pricing, and promotional bonuses — makes it very difficult for workers to see the decline. If a traditional employer cut wages by 30 percent over four years, we'd call it what it is. When an algorithm does it incrementally, it becomes invisible."

The Fight for Rights

Canadian gig workers are not waiting for the platforms to change. They are organizing.

Gig Workers United, a grassroots organization founded in Toronto in 2023, has grown to 3,400 members across six cities. Its founding coordinator, Nadia El-Masri, is herself a former delivery driver who was deactivated from two platforms after speaking publicly about working conditions.

"Deactivation is the gig economy's version of being fired," El-Masri said. "Except when you're fired from a real job, you can file for employment insurance. You can go to the labour board. When you're deactivated, you get an email from a no-reply address. There's no appeal. There's no severance. There's nothing."

Legal challenges are also making headway. In British Columbia, the Labour Relations Board ruled in September 2025 that delivery drivers for one major platform are employees, not independent contractors, and are therefore entitled to minimum wage protections, overtime pay, and workplace safety coverage under the BC Employment Standards Act. The platform is appealing. In Ontario, the Digital Platform Workers' Rights Act, passed in 2024, provides some protections — including minimum pay per assignment, 24-hour notice of deactivation, and a dispute resolution process — but stops short of classifying gig workers as employees.

"They call it flexibility. Flexibility for who? I can't choose my hours — the algorithm decides when it's busy enough to send me orders. I can't negotiate my pay. I can't refuse a bad order without being penalized. What exactly am I free to choose?"— Marcus Webb, delivery courier, Toronto

"The Ontario act is a floor, not a ceiling," said labour lawyer Fay Faraday, who has represented gig workers in several test cases. "It acknowledges that these workers deserve some protections. But by creating a separate category — 'digital platform workers' rather than 'employees' — it also enshrines a two-tier system. You have workers doing the same physical labour as employees, facing the same dangers, but with fewer rights. That's not equality. That's a legal fiction designed to protect a business model."

After the Accident

When we last spoke to Priya Kapoor in March 2026, she was preparing to return to delivery work. Her leg had healed enough that she could ride a bicycle, though she still felt pain on cold days. She had sold her damaged bicycle and bought a cheaper one. She could not afford to stay off the road any longer.

"People tell me I should find a different job," she said. "As if there are all these great jobs just sitting there for someone with a bachelor's degree from India that Canada doesn't recognize. This is the job. This is what's available. And they've designed it so that you have no choice but to accept whatever they offer, because the alternative is nothing."

She paused, adjusting the brace on her leg.

"I just want someone to be responsible. Not the algorithm. Not a chatbot. A person. A company. Someone who looks at what happened to me and says, 'This is not okay, and here's what we're going to do about it.' That's not radical. That's just basic. That's just what a job is supposed to be."

She opened the app, checked for available orders, and rode into the evening.
