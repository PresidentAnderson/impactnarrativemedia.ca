The Invisible Eviction: How Corporate Landlords Are Displacing 40,000 Canadian Families

A six-month investigation reveals the systematic tactics used to push low-income tenants out of affordable housing across five provinces — and the communities fighting back.

AO
By Amara Okafor
March 24, 2026 · 18 min read · Investigation
⚠ Content Advisory

This investigation contains accounts of housing loss, financial distress, and impacts on children and elderly residents. Descriptions of displacement-related anxiety and depression are included. If you or someone you know is experiencing a housing crisis, contact the Canada Housing Crisis Line: 1-800-668-2642.

On the morning she was told to leave the apartment where she had raised two children, buried a husband, and weathered three recessions, Doreen Whitfield sat at her kitchen table in Hamilton, Ontario, and stared at the eviction notice for eleven minutes before she could bring herself to read it. She counted the minutes later because the clock on the stove — the one her late husband Gerald had installed in 1997 — was directly in her line of sight.

"I kept thinking, this can't be real," said Whitfield, 74, who had lived in her two-bedroom unit at 445 Barton Street East for twelve years, paying $875 a month. "I know every crack in these walls. I know which floorboard creaks. This is my home."

Six weeks later, Whitfield was gone. Her building, a modest three-storey walk-up that had housed working-class families since 1962, was acquired by Hearthstone Capital Partners, a Toronto-based real estate investment trust that manages $1.4 billion in residential assets across five provinces. The company filed for above-guideline rent increases on all 24 units. When the increases were approved, rents rose from an average of $910 to $1,475 — a 62 percent jump. Fourteen families left within three months. The remaining ten were offered buyouts ranging from $2,000 to $5,000. By September 2025, the building was empty. It reopened four months later as "The Barton," featuring quartz countertops, in-unit laundry, and rents starting at $2,100.

Doreen Whitfield's story is not exceptional. It is, in fact, the template.

40,000
Canadian families displaced by corporate landlord tactics over the past three years, according to data compiled from provincial tribunal records, municipal housing registries, and tenant advocacy organizations across five provinces.

The Playbook: Five Tactics, Five Provinces

Over the past six months, Impact Narrative Media reviewed more than 3,200 landlord-tenant tribunal filings, interviewed 147 current and former tenants, spoke with 23 housing advocates and legal experts, and analyzed corporate acquisition records in Ontario, British Columbia, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and Manitoba. What emerged was a pattern so consistent that tenant lawyers have given it a name: the playbook.

The playbook has five core tactics, deployed in sequence or combination depending on the jurisdiction and the building's existing tenant protections.

Tactic one: the renoviction. A landlord serves notice that a unit requires renovations so extensive that the tenant must vacate. In Ontario, this is filed under Section 50 of the Residential Tenancies Act. In British Columbia, it falls under Section 49 of the Residential Tenancy Act. The tenant leaves. The "renovations" — often cosmetic, sometimes barely noticeable — are completed. The unit is re-listed at market rate, sometimes double or triple the original rent.

In Vancouver, Mei-Lin and David Chang, both 68, received a renoviction notice for their Kitsilano apartment in April 2025 after 19 years of tenancy. Their rent was $1,150 for a spacious one-bedroom with a view of English Bay. The stated renovations: new flooring, updated kitchen cabinets, and "modernized bathroom fixtures." The estimated cost, according to the landlord's filing, was $28,000. The unit was re-listed three months later at $2,650.

"They told us we could move back in at the new rent. That's not moving back in. That's a cruel joke. Our pension is $2,300 a month between the two of us."— Mei-Lin Chang, retired teacher, Vancouver

Tactic two: the above-guideline increase (AGI). In Ontario, landlords can apply to the Landlord and Tenant Board for rent increases above the provincial guideline — currently 2.5 percent — to cover capital expenditures. On paper, AGIs are meant for necessary repairs: a new roof, a failing elevator, a boiler replacement. In practice, our investigation found that corporate landlords are using them strategically, bundling cosmetic upgrades with necessary maintenance to justify increases of 8 to 15 percent, sometimes for three consecutive years.

Data obtained through freedom of information requests shows that AGI applications in Ontario increased 340 percent between 2021 and 2025. Of the 1,847 applications filed in 2025, 73 percent were filed by corporate entities — REITs, numbered companies, and private equity-backed firms — rather than individual landlords.

Tactic three: neglect and demolish. This is the slowest and most insidious approach. A corporate landlord acquires a building and allows it to deteriorate — delaying repairs, ignoring maintenance requests, letting common areas decay. As conditions worsen, tenants leave voluntarily. Once vacancy reaches a critical threshold, the landlord applies for a demolition permit, citing the building as beyond economic repair.

"The landlord didn't evict us. The black mould did. The broken elevator did. The rats did. They just waited."

In Winnipeg's West End, a 48-unit apartment complex on Sargent Avenue went from 92 percent occupancy to 15 percent over 18 months after its acquisition by Prairie Holdings Group, a subsidiary of a Calgary-based investment fund. Tenants documented 247 outstanding maintenance requests. The building was demolished in January 2026. A mixed-use development with rents starting at $1,800 is planned for the site.

Tactic four: the buyout. Sometimes called "cash for keys," this tactic involves offering tenants lump-sum payments to surrender their leases. Buyout offers documented in our investigation ranged from $1,500 to $15,000, with the average around $4,200 — roughly two to three months' rent in most markets. For tenants living paycheque to paycheque, the immediate cash is hard to refuse, even when it means losing a below-market unit they can never replace.

Tactic five: personal-use evictions. In Ontario, a landlord or their immediate family member can evict a tenant to occupy the unit themselves. Our analysis found that 38 percent of personal-use eviction filings by corporate entities in the Greater Toronto Area between 2023 and 2025 were followed by the unit being re-listed on the rental market within 12 months — a potential violation of the Act that carries a maximum fine of $50,000 for individuals and $250,000 for corporations, but which is rarely enforced.

The Human Cost: Three Families, Three Cities

Behind every data point is a family. We followed three across three cities as their housing disappeared beneath them.

Hamilton: The Whitfield family. Doreen Whitfield, whose story opens this investigation, spent four months in a women's shelter after losing her apartment. Her daughter, Keisha, 42, lives in a two-bedroom apartment in Stoney Creek with her own two children and could not take in her mother without violating her lease's occupancy limits. Whitfield was eventually placed in a subsidized seniors' unit in Brantford, 45 minutes from her doctor, her church, and the neighbourhood where she had lived for over a decade.

"I'm grateful for the roof," Whitfield said during our third interview, in January 2026. "But I lost my community. At my age, you don't just rebuild that."

Montreal: Sophie Tremblay-Laurent. A single mother of three, Tremblay-Laurent, 34, worked as a personal support worker at a long-term care facility in the Plateau-Mont-Royal, earning $22.50 an hour. She had lived in her $780-per-month apartment on Rue Rachel since 2019. In March 2025, her building was acquired by Domaine Québec Inc. Within two months, she received notice that extensive renovations would require her to vacate for "a minimum of eight months."

Under Quebec's Civil Code, tenants have the right to return after renovations at a rent increase that reflects the actual cost of work done. But Tremblay-Laurent, like many tenants in our investigation, was never informed of this right. She left and spent five months searching for an apartment she could afford within transit distance of her children's schools and her workplace. She ultimately moved to Laval, adding 90 minutes to her daily commute.

"I leave the house at 5:30 in the morning now. My oldest, Mathieu, he's 11 — he gets his sisters ready for school. An 11-year-old shouldn't have to do that. But what choice do I have?"— Sophie Tremblay-Laurent, Montreal

Vancouver: The Changs. After their renoviction, Mei-Lin and David Chang moved into their son's basement suite in Surrey. Mei-Lin, who has a chronic respiratory condition, found the basement's poor ventilation worsened her symptoms. David, who had been an active volunteer at the Kitsilano Neighbourhood House for 15 years, stopped going. "It's too far now," he said. "And honestly, I don't have the energy. Something broke in me when we had to leave."

Following the Money: Who Profits?

The financialization of Canadian housing — the transformation of homes from places people live into assets that generate returns for investors — has accelerated dramatically since the COVID-19 pandemic.

Our analysis of land title transfers, corporate registrations, and securities filings identified 14 corporate entities responsible for acquiring a combined 8,400 units of affordable rental housing across the five provinces studied. The largest, Hearthstone Capital Partners, added 2,200 units to its portfolio between 2023 and 2025, primarily in Ontario and Nova Scotia. Its annual report to investors boasts of "value-add acquisition strategies" that have delivered "average rent growth of 47 percent across repositioned assets."

In that language — "value-add," "repositioned" — lies the mechanism. Corporate landlords do not acquire affordable housing to keep it affordable. They acquire it precisely because it is affordable, because the gap between current rents and market rents represents unrealized profit.

"This is extraction," said Dr. Martine Agosto, a housing economist at the University of British Columbia and co-author of Financialized Housing and the Canadian Dream. "These firms are buying buildings where rents are low because long-term tenants have been paying below market. They then use every legal and quasi-legal tool available to remove those tenants and capture the spread. The building is the same building. The neighbourhood is the same neighbourhood. What's changed is who's allowed to live there."

The returns are substantial. Hearthstone's repositioned portfolio in Hamilton alone generated $14.2 million in incremental annual revenue — the difference between old rents and new rents across 340 units — on a capital expenditure of $6.8 million in renovations. That is a payback period of less than six months.

Dr. Ricardo Tranjan, a senior researcher at the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives and author of The Tenant Class, has been tracking this phenomenon for a decade. "What we're seeing is a transfer of wealth," Tranjan told Impact Narrative Media. "Money flows from the pockets of low-income tenants, who are forced into more expensive housing or homelessness, to the balance sheets of investment trusts, whose unitholders are overwhelmingly in the top income decile. It is a poverty-creation machine dressed up as real estate investment."

The Regulatory Failure

Every province has tenant protection laws. None of them are working well enough.

In Ontario, the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB) has a backlog of more than 53,000 cases. Average wait times for a hearing exceed eight months. During that wait, tenants facing above-guideline increases or dubious eviction notices continue to live in uncertainty, unable to plan, unable to fight effectively, unable to move on.

"The system is designed around the assumption that landlords and tenants have roughly equal resources," said Bahar Shadpour, a staff lawyer at the Advocacy Centre for Tenants Ontario (ACTO). "That assumption was always questionable. Now that we're dealing with billion-dollar REITs on one side and minimum-wage workers on the other, it's absurd. My clients are going up against legal teams that bill more per hour than they earn in a week."

In British Columbia, reforms introduced in 2021 — including restrictions on renovictions and requirements for landlords to offer equivalent units to displaced tenants — have been partially effective but contain loopholes that sophisticated operators exploit. In Quebec, the Régie du logement is similarly backlogged, and enforcement of return rights after renovations is inconsistent.

Nova Scotia, which only introduced rent control in 2020 as an emergency pandemic measure, has already begun loosening restrictions under industry pressure. Manitoba's protections remain among the weakest in the country.

Fighting Back: The Tenant Movement

In the face of systematic displacement, tenants are organizing.

ACORN Canada, the country's largest tenants' union, has grown from 15,000 members in 2020 to 42,000 in 2026. Its Hamilton chapter was instrumental in documenting the Barton Street evictions and bringing them to public attention. In Toronto, the Parkdale Organize collective has successfully blocked three AGI applications through coordinated legal challenges. In Vancouver, the Vancouver Tenants Union has established a "renoviction rapid response" network that connects newly served tenants with legal aid within 48 hours.

And in February 2026, a landmark ruling from the Ontario Divisional Court sent shockwaves through the industry. In Kaur v. Maple Leaf Residential REIT, Justice Carolyn Shapiro ruled that a pattern of personal-use evictions followed by re-listing at higher rents constituted "a systematic scheme to circumvent the protections of the Residential Tenancies Act" and awarded $180,000 in damages to the four applicant families — the largest such award in Ontario history.

"This ruling says what tenants have known all along: these aren't isolated bad actors. This is a business model. And it's illegal."— Bahar Shadpour, Staff Lawyer, Advocacy Centre for Tenants Ontario

The ruling is being appealed. Maple Leaf Residential REIT, in a statement to investors, called it "an unprecedented overreach that creates regulatory uncertainty in the Ontario rental housing market." But tenant advocates see it as a turning point.

"For the first time, a court has looked at the pattern, not just the individual case," said Alejandra Bravo, Ontario organizer for ACORN Canada. "That's everything. Because the whole playbook depends on each eviction looking like an isolated, legitimate action. When you zoom out and see the pattern, you see what it really is: displacement for profit."

What Needs to Change

Housing advocates, legal experts, and academics interviewed for this investigation converged on several policy recommendations:

Vacancy control: Currently, most provinces only limit rent increases for existing tenants. When a tenant leaves or is evicted, the landlord can set any rent they choose for the next tenant. Vacancy control — which limits rent increases between tenancies — would eliminate the financial incentive to displace tenants. It exists in Manitoba in limited form and has been proposed, but not implemented, in Ontario and British Columbia.

Corporate acquisition limits: Several experts recommended restricting or taxing the acquisition of existing affordable rental housing by corporate entities, particularly REITs, which benefit from tax advantages not available to individual landlords or non-profit housing providers.

Enforcement with teeth: Current penalties for illegal evictions are too low to deter corporate actors for whom fines are a cost of doing business. Shadpour advocates for penalties indexed to corporate revenue, not flat amounts, and for provincial enforcement units with the mandate and resources to investigate patterns of abuse proactively rather than waiting for individual tenant complaints.

Right to legal representation: In Ontario's LTB, landlords are represented by lawyers in 89 percent of hearings. Tenants have legal representation in only 12 percent. "You cannot have a fair system where one side has a team of lawyers and the other side has a printout from Google," said Tranjan.

Doreen Whitfield's New View

In her new apartment in Brantford, Doreen Whitfield has hung the same curtains she had on Barton Street — cream-coloured with small yellow flowers, bought at a church bazaar in 2015. The stove is different. It doesn't have a clock.

"I'm 74 years old," she said. "I worked at the Dofasco plant for 22 years. I paid my taxes. I raised my kids. I did everything you're supposed to do. And a company I'd never heard of, run by people I'll never meet, decided my home was worth more without me in it."

She paused.

"I want people to know this is happening. Not just to me. To thousands of us. And nobody's stopping it."
