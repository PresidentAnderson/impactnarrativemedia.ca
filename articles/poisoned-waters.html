Poisoned Waters: First Nations Communities Still Waiting for Clean Drinking Water After 30 Years of Promises

Despite billions in federal commitments, 28 long-term drinking water advisories remain in effect. The people living under them tell their story.

JM
By James Makokis
March 22, 2026 · 15 min read · Environment
⚠ Content Advisory

This article discusses the ongoing effects of colonialism on Indigenous communities, including health impacts on children and elders. It includes references to intergenerational trauma. We have worked with Indigenous consultants and community members in the development of this story.

Support resources: Indian Residential Schools Crisis Line: 1-866-925-4419 · Hope for Wellness Helpline: 1-855-242-3310

The water in Neskantaga First Nation has not been safe to drink since 1995. That is not a typo. For more than thirty years — through six prime ministers, eleven federal budgets that mentioned Indigenous water infrastructure, and $3.8 billion in announced funding — the 300 residents of this remote Oji-Cree community in northwestern Ontario have been unable to turn on their taps and drink.

Chief Wayne Moonias stands beside the community's water treatment plant, a squat concrete building with rusted vents and a cracked foundation. The plant was built in 1993. It was inadequate when it was commissioned. Three decades later, it is a monument to broken promises.

"Every government says they'll fix it," Moonias told Impact Narrative Media during our visit in January 2026. "Chretien said it. Martin said it. Harper said it. Trudeau made it his signature promise. And now we're hearing the same words from a new government. At some point, the words stop meaning anything. At some point, you just want to be able to give your grandchild a glass of water from the sink."

28
Long-term drinking water advisories remain in effect across First Nations communities in Canada as of March 2026, according to Indigenous Services Canada. A "long-term" advisory is one that has been in place for more than one year. Some have persisted for decades.

Thirty Years of Promises

The timeline of federal commitments on Indigenous water reads like an exercise in repetition.

In 2003, the National Assessment of First Nations Water and Wastewater Systems identified 218 communities with "high-risk" water systems. The federal government pledged $600 million over five years. In 2008, an expert panel found that conditions had worsened, not improved, and recommended $4.7 billion in investment. The government committed to $330 million. In 2015, Prime Minister Justin Trudeau campaigned on ending all long-term drinking water advisories on First Nations reserves by March 2021. By that deadline, 38 advisories remained. The target was moved to 2026.

As of this writing, 28 advisories remain.

"The government will tell you they've lifted 144 advisories since 2015, and that's true," said Dr. Kellie Leitch, a researcher at the Yellowhead Institute who tracks water infrastructure commitments. "What they don't tell you is that 45 new advisories were issued in the same period, that many 'lifted' advisories are fragile — dependent on temporary fixes that could fail — and that the remaining 28 are the hardest, most underfunded, most remote communities. These are the ones that have been waiting the longest."

The Communities Still Waiting

Neskantaga First Nation, Ontario. Thirty-one years under a boil-water advisory. The community of 300 was temporarily evacuated in October 2020 after oily sheen was discovered in the water reservoir. They returned weeks later to the same system. A new water treatment plant has been in planning since 2018. Construction has been delayed four times. The current projected completion date is late 2027.

"My daughter is 28 years old," said community member Rena Moonias-Okeese. "She has never, in her entire life, had clean tap water. She grew up thinking bottled water was just how it works. I had to explain to her that in Toronto, in Ottawa, in any city where non-Indigenous people live, you just turn on the tap. She didn't believe me at first."

"My daughter is 28 years old. She has never, in her entire life, had clean tap water. She grew up thinking bottled water was just how it works."— Rena Moonias-Okeese, Neskantaga First Nation

Shoal Lake 40 First Nation, Ontario/Manitoba border. This community of 270 sits on the source of Winnipeg's drinking water. For more than two decades, while the City of Winnipeg drew clean water from Shoal Lake through an aqueduct built in 1919, the community on that same lake could not drink its own water. A boil-water advisory was in effect from 1997 to 2021. Although the advisory was technically lifted after a new water treatment plant was completed, community members report ongoing concerns about system maintenance and the capacity of the single operator trained to run the plant.

"Winnipeg drinks our lake and calls it pristine," said Elder Margaret Chicken, 73. "We drink the same lake and get sick. Explain that to me. I've been asking for 25 years and nobody can explain it."

Grassy Narrows First Nation, Ontario. The mercury crisis in Grassy Narrows — where Dryden Chemical Company dumped 9,000 kilograms of mercury into the English-Wabigoon river system between 1962 and 1970 — is one of the worst industrial poisonings in Canadian history. More than fifty years later, mercury levels in the river system remain elevated. The fish that once sustained the community are contaminated. Mercury poisoning has caused neurological damage, birth defects, and chronic illness across three generations.

A mercury treatment centre, promised since 2017, broke ground in 2023. It is not yet operational.

The Health Toll

The consequences of unsafe water are not abstract. They are measured in skin rashes on children. In E. coli infections that send elders to hospitals hours away. In the anxiety of parents boiling water for formula at 3 a.m. and wondering if they boiled it long enough. In the gastrointestinal illnesses that are so common in some communities that residents have stopped reporting them.

Dr. Anna Banerji, an infectious disease specialist at the University of Toronto who has worked in northern Indigenous communities for 18 years, described what she has witnessed.

"I've treated children with skin infections that are directly attributable to bathing in contaminated water. I've seen rates of gastrointestinal illness in some communities that are 26 times the national average. I've seen pregnant women who cannot access clean water for drinking or cooking. In any other context, in any non-Indigenous community, this would be treated as a public health emergency. Here, it's treated as a chronic condition. Something to be managed, not solved."

$3.8 Billion
Federal funding announced for Indigenous water infrastructure since 2016. According to the Parliamentary Budget Officer, approximately $1.2 billion of this remains unspent or has been reallocated to other priorities.

Where Did the Money Go?

This is the question that haunts every community still under advisory.

Federal budgets since 2016 have allocated $3.8 billion specifically for water and wastewater infrastructure on First Nations reserves. Yet the Parliamentary Budget Officer's 2025 review found that only $2.1 billion had been spent. Of the remainder, approximately $500 million was reallocated to other Indigenous Services Canada priorities, $400 million was "re-profiled" — bureaucratic language for delayed to future fiscal years — and $800 million remains in various stages of approval, procurement, or planning.

The problem, experts say, is not simply funding. It is the structure of how that funding flows.

"The federal model requires communities to apply for project-specific funding through a competitive process that demands engineering assessments, environmental reviews, and project management capacity that many remote communities simply don't have," said Christy Smith, a water policy analyst at the Assembly of First Nations. "You're asking a community of 300 people with two administrative staff to compete for infrastructure dollars using the same bureaucratic framework as a mid-sized city. It's designed to fail."

When projects are approved, they are often underfunded. A 2024 report by the First Nations Financial Management Board found that 60 percent of completed water projects were built to minimum standards that leave no margin for population growth, equipment failure, or changing source water conditions. Operation and maintenance funding — the money needed to keep systems running after they are built — covers an average of 69 percent of actual costs, leaving communities to cover the gap from already-strained budgets.

Water Sovereignty: A Different Vision

Increasingly, Indigenous communities and organizations are rejecting the federal framework altogether and asserting water sovereignty — the right to manage, protect, and govern their own water systems according to Indigenous law and values.

The Anishinabek Nation's Declaration of Water Sovereignty, issued in 2024, asserts that "water is not a resource to be managed. Water is a relative to be respected. Our laws have governed our relationship with water since time immemorial, and those laws must be the foundation of any solution."

Autumn Peltier, the Anishinaabe water protector from Wikwemikong First Nation who has been advocating for clean water since she was eight years old, spoke at the United Nations in September 2025. She is now 21.

"We keep asking Canada to give us clean water. Maybe we should stop asking. Maybe we should start governing. Our water, our laws, our systems, built by our people, for our people."— Autumn Peltier, Anishinaabe water protector

Several communities are pursuing this path. Lubicon Lake Band in Alberta has partnered with Engineers Without Borders to build a community-designed and community-operated water system that integrates traditional knowledge with modern engineering. The Mohawk Council of Kahnawake, near Montreal, operates its own water utility independent of federal funding and maintains water quality that consistently exceeds federal standards.

"The solutions exist," said Chief Moonias of Neskantaga. "The engineering exists. The knowledge exists. What's missing is the political will to treat this as what it is: a human rights crisis in one of the richest countries on earth."

A Glass of Water

Before we left Neskantaga, Rena Moonias-Okeese showed us the shelf in her kitchen where she keeps the bottled water. Three cases of 24, stacked against the wall. She goes through about four cases a week for her family of five. The water is flown in by cargo plane. It costs roughly four times what it would cost at a grocery store in Thunder Bay, 430 kilometres to the south.

"People ask me what I want," she said. "They expect some big political answer. I don't have one. I just want to give my kids a glass of water from the tap. That's it. That's all I've ever wanted."

Outside, it was minus 32. The lake was frozen solid. Beneath the ice, the water was clean. It has always been clean. It is only the systems built to deliver it that have failed.

This article was reported over four months with the cooperation of community members in Neskantaga, Shoal Lake 40, and Grassy Narrows First Nations. All interviews were conducted with informed consent.
